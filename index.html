Autobiography

Held by the Overlooked

For much of my life, I worried that my drifting attention would keep me from ever becoming a productive person. Design showed me the opposite: my habit of noticing what others overlook gives my attention purpose.

On walks with my mom in the hills above my childhood home in Berkeley, I remembered not the obvious landmarks but the cracks in a stone bridge forming accidental patterns and torn posters weathered into soft, muted color. When I pointed them out, my mom responded with surprise and delight, as if she were seeing the world briefly through my eyes. Her reaction showed me that my attention to overlooked details was not strange, but perceptive.

I now practice that fascination deliberately by gathering scraps, scanning surfaces, and building collages from fragments that might otherwise disappear. What once felt like misplaced attention now feels like an important part of my artistic process. I am drawn to details like worn textures, torn edges, and traces of age because they can give an image mood, history, and emotional weight, making it feel intimate or lived-in. Paying attention to those details gives me both material and inspiration when I begin assembling my own visual work, whether in digital design or physical collage.

Because this habit trained me to pay attention to how people notice, move, and remember, it naturally led me to design fields that shape people's experiences of spaces, visual information, and objects. UX/UI design shapes how people move through digital spaces, environmental graphic design shapes how they move through physical ones, and exhibition design shapes how they interpret objects and stories. These fields all shape attention and determine what stays with people after an experience ends. Whether through a screen, an exhibition, or a designed space, I am drawn to work that does more than just communicate “necessary” information. I want to create experiences that invite close attention and deep engagement.

Product Line Visual Identity Mockup

I designed a cohesive product line mockup in Photoshop to show how one visual identity can scale across multiple touchpoints. I built a consistent typographic system and color story, applied it across the set, then arranged everything for clear hierarchy and quick readability so it works as a marketing-ready visual. To finish, I used compositing techniques (alignment, perspective, texture, and realistic shadows/lighting) to unify the scene and make the products feel believable and brand-consistent.
